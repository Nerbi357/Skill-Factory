# Research conduct

**What changes because of this skill:** without it, an investigation produces a
careful, well-qualified pile of facts and leaves the reader to assemble a decision
out of it — which is how two equally scrupulous sessions can reach opposite
conclusions from the same evidence. With it, the report ends where a decision can
be made from it.

**Covers:** how an investigation is run and reported.
**Leaves out:** how confidently any single fact is stated — that is
`confidence-check`, used throughout this skill and not repeated in it. Also whether
a specific data source is fit to build on, which is its own question.

---

## 1. Start from the decision, not the question

Before searching, say what the answer will be used for. "Can we get funding amounts
for these companies" is a question; "we need to decide whether to build a second
ingest path this month" is the decision behind it. The decision determines what
counts as enough — a question researched without it expands forever, because
nothing marks the point where more evidence stops changing anything.

Write the decision down first. If it cannot be named, ask before spending the
hours.

## 2. End with what you would do

**This is the section the skill exists for.** Marks alone do not make a decision. A
report scrupulously honest about every fact and silent about what to do with them
hands the whole judgement back untouched — the failure mode this discipline
invites, because carefulness feels like completion.

Finish with three lines:

- **What I would do on this evidence** — the recommendation, plainly.
- **What it rests on** — which of the marked facts it actually depends on. Usually
  two or three out of forty, and naming them tells the reader where to aim their
  scepticism.
- **What would change it** — the single check that would most move the answer.

The third line is the one that pays. It turns an open question into a task with a
known cost, so a gap stops being a permanent asterisk and becomes something someone
can go and close.

When the evidence genuinely does not support a recommendation, **that is the
recommendation** — say it outright rather than leaving it to be inferred from a wall
of qualified facts.

## 3. Report the shape of the evidence, not just its content

Two facts of equal importance can rest on very different footings, and the reader
cannot see that from the facts alone. Say up front what you were able to reach and
what you were not, and what that does to the whole report. One paragraph.

This is not the same as marking individual claims — that happens per claim, and it
is `confidence-check`'s job. This is the reader's map of how much weight the
document as a whole will bear.

## 4. Reconcile disagreement rather than picking a side

When sources conflict, say they conflict, say which you would believe and why, and
leave both visible. Silently choosing one and reporting it as settled destroys the
one piece of information a reader most needs: that the point is contested.

Where a conflict cannot be resolved, it is a candidate for §2's third line — an
unresolved disagreement between sources is usually the cheapest thing to go and
settle.

## 5. Stop when more evidence stops changing the decision

Not when the question feels exhausted — questions never do. The stopping rule comes
from §1: once the next check could not plausibly change what you would recommend,
the research is finished, and continuing is procrastination wearing a lab coat.

Say which checks you deliberately did not run and why. That is different from §2's
third line, which names the one check that *would* matter.

## Owner preferences

- **He reads reports for decisions, not for completeness.** Structure: what was
  done → what was found → the numbers proving it → what is needed from him.
- **Depth by default is the result and what it means for him**, not the internals.
- **A clean negative is a good outcome.** A measured "this does not work" is a
  result, not a failure, and it is cheaper than a hedged maybe.

## Open questions for the owner

**Does §1 belong here or in a skill about starting any piece of work?** "Name the
decision before spending the hours" is not specific to research. If a broader
skill about beginning a task ever exists, this moves there.

**Is this one skill or two?** Running an investigation and writing it up are
arguably different jobs — the first is method, the second is output shape. They are
together here because the stopping rule in §5 depends on the decision named in §1,
which is also what §2 reports against. Splitting them would cut that thread.
